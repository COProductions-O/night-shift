# Portable Acoustic Treatment for Apartment Guitar Recording in Belgium and the Netherlands: Renter-Friendly Techniques for Cleaner Demos

Most guitarists in Belgian and Dutch apartments already know the quiet-recording playbook: DI into an interface, amp simulations, closed-back headphones, and late-night volume discipline. Those techniques keep the neighbours happy. They do not automatically produce a clean recording. An untreated living room or bedroom still adds its own boxy reflections, flutter echoes and low-mid mud to every acoustic guitar or vocal take. That extra room sound is exactly what makes a demo sound “home-recorded” even when the performance is solid.

The good news is that meaningful improvement does not require permanent wall panels or landlord permission. Portable, non-destructive treatment lets you reduce the worst reflections only while you are recording, then pack everything away. The goal is not a perfectly dead studio. It is a recording that is dry enough, clear enough and free of obvious room problems that a producer or engineer can work with it quickly. At typical Belgian and Dutch studio rates of €50–100+ per hour (or day packages in the €350–600 range), every cleaner take you deliver at home is money and time saved.

## Soundproofing versus Treatment: What You Can Actually Do in a Rental

Soundproofing tries to stop sound leaving the room (mass, decoupling, sealing gaps). In a typical BE/NL apartment this is expensive, often impossible without major works, and rarely approved by landlords or VvE rules. Treatment is different: it controls the sound that stays inside the room so the microphone hears more of the instrument and less of the walls. Absorption is the practical tool for apartment players.

Thin acoustic foam does little below roughly 500 Hz. Thicker materials and air gaps help more with low-mids, but even simple thick blankets and rugs already tame the flutter and harsh early reflections that ruin acoustic guitar and vocal clarity. For electric guitar recorded via DI you mainly need a decent monitoring environment or headphones; the room treatment still matters the moment you add any acoustic instrument or a vocal.

## Zero-Cost and Low-Cost Starting Points

Before buying anything, use what you already own.

- Heavy winter blankets, duvets or moving blankets hung over a clothes airer, chair backs or a simple frame of mic stands create an instant absorption zone behind or beside the microphone. Moving blankets (often sold in packs at hardware stores) are particularly dense.
- A full clothes closet with the door open is one of the best free “booths” available in most apartments. The hanging clothes absorb mid and high frequencies; the open door prevents excessive bass build-up. Place the microphone just inside the doorway and the guitarist just outside or partially inside.
- A thick rug under the player reduces floor reflections. Bookshelves filled with books, sofas and mattresses leaning against walls all add useful absorption.
- Soft furnishings already in the room can be rearranged for a session. Move the sofa behind the mic or drag the mattress from the bed and lean it against the nearest hard wall.

These methods cost nothing extra and can be set up and torn down in minutes. Many professional engineers still use variations of the “blanket fort” approach when tracking in untreated hotel rooms or temporary spaces.

## Affordable Portable Gear Available in Belgium and the Netherlands

Once the free options have shown what a difference even modest absorption makes, a few targeted purchases improve consistency and convenience. Bax Music (Belgium and Netherlands) and Thomann stock the relevant items with next-day or short delivery across the region.

Reflection filters (also called vocal shields or isolation filters) are the single most useful dedicated item for many apartment players. A basic Devine RF 20 or similar aluminium-and-foam model sits on a mic stand behind the microphone and cuts a large portion of rear-wall reflections. Prices for solid entry-level units typically fall in the €40–70 range at Bax. Higher-end multi-layer designs such as the sE Electronics RF-X sit higher (€150–250) and colour the sound less. Either is portable, requires no wall mounting, and works for both vocals and close-miked acoustic guitar.

Acoustic cloth and heavy stage drapes are another practical option. Adam Hall and Ayra molton or reflex cloths (3 × 3 m pieces often around €100–150) can be hung on a portable frame or over existing furniture. They absorb more evenly than thin foam and can be stored folded.

Stand-mounted or freestanding absorber panels exist in the t.akustik, Auralex and Devine ranges. Modular wall sections that clip together or hang on stands let you build a temporary three-sided booth around the player and microphone, then fold or roll them away. Expect to spend €100–300 for a useful starter set rather than a full room kit.

Full Primacoustic or Auralex room kits (€400–1000+) are excellent if you own the space or have a dedicated room, but they are overkill and often impractical for renters. Start with one reflection filter plus blankets; add portable panels only if the first steps leave obvious problems.

## Placement That Actually Matters

Coverage percentage is less important than covering the first reflection points for the microphone.

1. Behind the microphone is the highest-priority zone. A reflection filter or a hanging blanket here removes the strongest early reflection.
2. To the sides of the player, especially if the walls are close and parallel. Even a duvet over a clothes rack helps.
3. Above the microphone if the ceiling is low and hard. A temporary cloud made from a blanket on a frame or a portable panel on a boom stand works.
4. Avoid completely deadening the space. Leave some liveliness; a totally dry closet recording can sound boxy in a different way. The closet-with-open-door method often strikes a good balance.

For acoustic guitar, the classic close-mic positions (12th-fret area, or a combination of soundhole and neck) still apply. The treatment simply reduces the room contribution so those positions translate more cleanly. For vocals, a reflection filter plus a pop filter and consistent distance is usually enough for a usable demo.

## Workflow for Better Apartment Demos

- Set up the treatment first, then set levels. A drier room lets you use less compression and EQ later.
- Record a short test phrase and listen back on headphones. If you still hear obvious slap-back or boxiness, add more absorption behind or beside the mic before committing to a full take.
- For singer-songwriter work, track guitar and vocal separately when possible. The treatment setup can stay the same; only the microphone position changes.
- Export clean, dry files (or lightly processed amp-sim guitar) with clear labelling. Include a simple note about the treatment used (“closet + RF20, dry”) so the engineer knows what they are receiving.
- Keep a small “session kit” (reflection filter, one or two blankets, clips or ties) in a bag or under the bed so setup takes less than five minutes.

These steps sit alongside the DI, amp-sim, headphone and click-track practices already covered in earlier pieces. Together they produce demos that need fewer corrective moves in the studio and leave more of the paid hours for creative decisions rather than fixing room problems.

## When the Apartment Still Isn’t Enough

Portable treatment improves clarity and reduces obvious room artefacts. It does not turn a 12 m² bedroom into a professional live room. Large dynamic range, detailed fingerstyle acoustic work, or final vocal takes for a release still benefit from a treated studio. The hybrid approach remains the most cost-effective for most BE/NL players: capture solid, dry performances at home with portable treatment, then use the studio for the parts that truly need the room, the microphones or the engineer’s ears.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Identify the closest hard surfaces to your usual mic position
- [ ] Test the open-closet method and a simple blanket behind the mic
- [ ] Add a reflection filter (€40–70 class) if rear-wall reflections remain obvious
- [ ] Place a thick rug under the player
- [ ] Record a short A/B test (treated vs untreated) and listen on headphones
- [ ] Store the portable pieces so they can be deployed in under five minutes
- [ ] Note the setup in the file comments when sending to a studio or collaborator

## Final Thoughts

Apartment recording in Belgium and the Netherlands will always be a compromise between neighbour peace and recording quality. Portable acoustic treatment is one of the highest-leverage compromises available. It costs little, leaves no marks on the walls, and directly improves the raw material you bring to a professional session. Cleaner demos mean less time spent fighting the room and more time spent on the music—exactly the outcome that justifies the studio rate when you finally book it.
