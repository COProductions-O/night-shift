# Home Mixing Basics for Guitar Tracks: Making Apartment Demos Sound Professional for Studios and Collaborators in Belgium and the Netherlands

**Meta description:** Practical, quiet-friendly mixing steps for guitarists in Belgian and Dutch apartments. Learn gain staging, EQ cuts, light compression, panning double-tracks, and clean export so your DI or amp-sim demos arrive at studios or collaborators sounding ready to use.

In dense Belgian and Dutch cities—Antwerp, Brussels, Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Ghent—most guitarists record at home because real amp volume is rarely an option after 22:00. The earlier articles in this series covered quiet DI and acoustic capture. Once those tracks exist, the next bottleneck is usually the mix: muddy low end, competing doubles, or levels that clip when the engineer opens the file. A clean home mix does not replace a professional studio session, but it shortens the time spent fixing problems once you get there and makes remote collaboration smoother.

This guide focuses on the minimum moves that turn usable apartment recordings into files a studio or collaborator can work with immediately. The techniques work in any DAW (Logic Pro, Ableton Live, Studio One, REAPER, Pro Tools, or the free versions that often ship with interfaces). They assume you already have clean DI or amp-sim tracks from the methods described in previous pieces.

## Start with Levels and Gain Staging

Before any EQ or compression, set levels so nothing is clipping and the overall balance is sensible. Aim for individual guitar tracks that peak around –12 to –6 dBFS when the part is at its loudest. This leaves headroom for the engineer and prevents the digital clipping that is hard to undo later.

If you recorded DI and are using an amp simulator, check the input gain into the simulator itself. Too much drive at that stage creates fizzy distortion that EQ cannot fully remove. A useful starting point is to set the raw DI so its peaks sit around –12 to –6 dBFS, then dial the amp model’s gain to taste. Many free or low-cost amp sims (including those bundled with Focusrite or Universal Audio interfaces common in BE/NL) respond well to this conservative approach.

For the overall session, leave the master fader at 0 dB and keep the mix peaking no higher than –3 dBFS until you are ready to export. This is especially helpful when sending files for remote sessions—engineers in Belgian or Dutch studios often work at lower monitoring levels and prefer headroom.

## EQ: Cut First, Boost Sparingly

Guitar tracks, especially those recorded with amp sims in untreated apartments, often carry excess low-mid energy (around 200–400 Hz) that makes the mix sound boxy. A high-pass filter is the single most useful tool. Set it between 80 and 120 Hz on electric guitar rhythm parts; go a little lower (60–80 Hz) only if the part is a solo or needs weight. Acoustic guitars can usually tolerate a high-pass around 70–100 Hz depending on the mic and room.

Next, listen for mud. A gentle cut of 2–4 dB between 250 and 400 Hz often clears space without making the guitar thin. On the presence side, a small boost (1–3 dB) between 2 and 4 kHz can help pick attack and clarity, but only if the part needs to cut through other instruments. Avoid large boosts; they tend to exaggerate any harshness already present from the amp sim or room reflections.

Double-tracked rhythm guitars benefit from slight EQ differences between the left and right takes. One track can keep a bit more midrange body while the other is brighter. This reduces the “comb-filter” effect that appears when two nearly identical tracks are panned hard left and right.

## Light Compression for Control

Compression on home guitar tracks should be subtle. A ratio of 3:1 or 4:1 with a medium attack (10–30 ms) and a release that recovers before the next note is usually enough. The goal is to catch the loudest peaks so the part sits more consistently, not to squash the dynamics.

For rhythm parts, aim for 2–4 dB of gain reduction on the loudest strums. Lead lines can take a little more if you want sustain, but keep the attack slow enough that the initial pick transient remains clear. Many stock compressors in Logic, Ableton, or Studio One work well for this; you do not need expensive plugins for the first pass.

If the track already has a lot of amp-sim compression or saturation, skip the plugin compressor entirely and rely on volume automation for any remaining level issues. Over-compressing a already-compressed DI tone is a common way home mixes start to sound flat.

## Panning Double-Tracks and Creating Width

Double-tracking is one of the most effective ways to make apartment recordings sound larger. Record the same part twice with slightly different amp or pickup settings, then pan one hard left and one hard right. The slight differences in performance and tone create natural width without the phase problems that can appear when you simply duplicate and delay a single track.

Keep lead lines and important melodic parts closer to the center so they remain clear when the mix is played in mono (common on phone speakers or club systems). Ambient or textural guitar parts can sit further out, around 50–75 % left or right, filling the sides without competing with the core rhythm.

When working in an apartment, you are almost always mixing on headphones. Check the panning in mono occasionally by collapsing the mix. If a guitar part disappears or becomes thin, the two sides are too similar or out of phase. Adjust EQ differences or performance variations until the mono version still holds up.

## Subtle Space: Reverb and Delay

A short room or plate reverb (decay under 1 second) can add a sense of place without washing out the guitar. Send the guitars to a shared reverb bus at a low level so the effect is felt more than heard. Delay is useful for leads—a slap-back (80–120 ms) or a short dotted eighth can add rhythm without cluttering the arrangement.

Because most apartment mixes are done on headphones, it is easy to overdo spatial effects. Listen at a moderate volume and compare the mix with and without the reverb. If the guitar feels farther away than you intended, reduce the send level. When the goal is to send clean files to a studio, many engineers prefer dry or lightly processed tracks so they can place the guitar in the full mix later.

## Export and Delivery Tips for Studios and Collaborators

When you are happy with the balance, bounce the guitar tracks (or the full mix if that is what was requested) as 24-bit WAV files at the session sample rate (usually 44.1 or 48 kHz). Include a short note with the tempo, key, and any special requests. If the studio prefers stems, export the left and right double-tracks separately rather than a stereo mix of just the guitars.

Name files clearly: “SongTitle_Guitar_Rhythm_L_Take2.wav”. This small habit saves time when the engineer in Antwerp or Utrecht opens the session. If you recorded DI, also send the dry DI tracks alongside the amp-sim versions; many studios can reamp or replace the tone if needed.

Typical quiet-hour realities in BE/NL (most municipalities enforce rest periods roughly 22:00–07:00, with local variations and apartment-specific VvE/VME rules) mean that late-night mixing sessions are usually done on headphones. That is fine for these basic decisions. If you later want a more accurate picture of the low end, check the bounce on a second set of headphones or a quiet pair of small speakers during daytime hours.

## When the Professional Studio Still Adds Clear Value

A well-prepared home mix gets you further into the session, but it does not replace the acoustic environment, monitoring, and experience of a professional room. Studios in Belgium and the Netherlands still offer advantages for final guitar tones that need real amp interaction, complex multi-mic setups, or the ability to compare several cabinets quickly. The preparation steps above simply mean less time is spent fixing basic problems and more time is available for creative choices.

If your goal is a release-ready track or a full band production, budget for at least a few hours of studio time after the home demos are solid. The files you send will already sit in a sensible balance, which is exactly what most engineers prefer to receive.

## Quick Checklist Before You Send Files

- Peaks on individual guitar tracks between –12 and –6 dBFS
- High-pass filter in place (80–120 Hz electric, 70–100 Hz acoustic)
- Light compression only if peaks are uneven (2–4 dB reduction)
- Double-tracks panned hard left/right with slight EQ or tone differences
- Mono check performed
- 24-bit WAV export with clear filenames and a short session note
- Dry DI included if it was recorded

These steps take little extra time once they become habit and turn quiet apartment work into material that studios and collaborators can use immediately. The result is fewer revisions, shorter sessions, and better final tracks—exactly the practical outcome most guitarists in Belgium and the Netherlands are looking for.
