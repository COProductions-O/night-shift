Many guitarists in Belgium and the Netherlands already own an audio interface and know how to capture a quiet DI track, yet they still hesitate to open a full digital audio workstation. The software feels heavy, the menus look technical, and the free options seem limited. In practice, a well-chosen free DAW is the missing piece that turns isolated practice takes into organised, studio-ready demos. ## Why a dedicated DAW still matters in 2026

A multi-effects pedal or a simple phone recorder can capture ideas, but neither gives you multiple takes, easy editing, click-track alignment, or clean export of separate DI and processed tracks. Those capabilities matter when you want to double a rhythm part, fix a timing slip before the studio day, or hand an engineer files that start at the same point and need almost no cleanup. Previous articles in this series already cover interfaces, latency fixes, amp-sim plugins, and the exact export checklist for studio delivery. The DAW is the environment that makes those steps practical instead of theoretical.

## The free options that actually work for apartment guitarists

As of late 2026 the strongest cross-platform choice is **Tracktion Waveform Free** (currently version 14.1). It offers unlimited audio and MIDI tracks, full VST/VST3/AU plugin support, no trial timer, and no export restrictions. It runs on Windows 10/11, macOS 13 or later (Intel and Apple Silicon), and Linux. Built-in tools include basic EQ, compression, delay, reverb, an impulse-response loader, and a simple drum sampler. The recent 14.1 update added an AI-assisted mix-analysis tool that can flag loudness, true-peak, and masking issues—useful for quick checks before you send a demo, without replacing proper human listening.

On Mac and iOS, **GarageBand** remains the easiest starting point. It is free, pre-installed or available from the App Store, and includes usable guitar amp models. Many players later move to Logic Pro, but GarageBand is already enough to record clean DI takes, layer a simple arrangement, and export a rough mix.

Windows users also have the free tier of Cakewalk Sonar (or Cakewalk Next), which requires a BandLab account. It is capable, but Waveform Free is generally simpler for pure guitar work and does not depend on an external account for basic offline use.

Avoid browser-only tools for serious guitar demos if you need low-latency monitoring and third-party amp sims; desktop software with ASIO or Core Audio remains more reliable in apartment conditions.

## First-time setup that avoids common frustration

1. Download Waveform Free directly from Tracktion’s official site (create a free account if asked). Install the Free edition, not a Pro trial.
2. Connect your interface (Scarlett 2i2 or similar in the €100–200 range remains common) and install its latest driver.
3. In Waveform’s audio settings, select the interface as the device, set the sample rate to 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz (match what you will use later in the studio), and start with a buffer of 128 or 256 samples. Lower buffers reduce latency but raise CPU load; test until monitoring feels immediate without crackles.
4. Create a new project. Add an audio track, name it “Guitar DI”, and set its input to the interface channel your guitar is plugged into.
5. Enable input monitoring and set the interface gain so the loudest peaks sit around –12 dB to –6 dB. This headroom prevents clipping when you later add amp-sim plugins.

If you hear double monitoring or a noticeable delay, revisit the latency article in this series for direct-monitoring and buffer advice. Closed-back headphones are essential so you can hear the amp sim without waking neighbours.

## Recording and arranging a useful guitar demo

Once the track is armed, record a short phrase against the built-in click or a simple drum loop from the Micro Drum Sampler. Waveform supports take recording and basic comping, so you can keep three or four attempts and edit the best bars together. For double-tracking, create a second track, pan the two DI recordings slightly left and right, and apply the same amp-sim settings to both. The double-tracking article explains the performance side; the DAW simply makes the technical side painless.

Load a free or low-cost amp simulator (Neural Amp Modeler profiles, free TONE3000 packs, or the plugins already discussed in the amp-sim guide). Insert it on the guitar track after recording the clean DI so you always retain the dry signal. Many players record the DI first, then experiment with tones offline—this keeps the apartment quiet and lets you try multiple cabinets without re-recording.

Keep arrangements simple: one or two rhythm tracks, a bass line if needed (DI is fine), a rough vocal or guide, and a reference click. Export stems early so you can hear how the parts sit together.

## Exporting files that studios actually want

When the demo is ready, bounce or render the guitar tracks as 24-bit WAV files at the project sample rate. Include both the dry DI and the amp-sim version if the engineer might reamp. Name files clearly (SongTitle_GuitarRhythm_DI_v1.wav). Leave several seconds of silence at the start so everything lines up. The dedicated article on delivering clean DI tracks gives the full checklist; following it means the studio spends the €50–100+ hourly rate on creative decisions instead of fixing alignment or clipping.

Waveform Free’s updated render window makes LUFS normalisation and background exports straightforward. Use it for a quick loudness check, then decide whether a professional mix is still needed.

## Common pitfalls and how to avoid them

- Plugin overload on older laptops: freeze or bounce the amp-sim track once the tone is final so CPU stays free for monitoring.
- Forgetting to save the project regularly: Waveform auto-saves, but still keep a manual backup on an external drive following the 3-2-1 rule covered earlier.
- Mismatched sample rates: set the rate once at the start of the project and leave it.
- Over-processing: a free DAW is excellent for organisation and rough mixes; leave heavy EQ and compression for the studio or a dedicated mixing session.
